Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Gabriel Perez, Gian Robles, Vedika Goyal, Mark Bishoff — gip005@ucsd.edu, gcrobles@ucsd.edu, vngoyal@ucsd.edu, mbishoff@ucsd.edu

Proposition: U.S. CO₂ emissions from burning fossil fuels are no longer a growing climate problem.

Dataset: IMF Climate Change Indicators, United States. Each visualization stacks two charts built from two of its indicators. The top chart uses CO₂ Emissions embodied in Domestic Final Demand, Production, and Trade, which comes from the OECD Trade in Embodied CO₂ (TeCO₂) database and counts CO₂ from fuel combustion only. The bottom chart uses Fossil Fuel Subsidies (IMF, 2023 update), whose implicit subsidies include the unpriced climate cost of each fuel's CO₂; its values for 2023–2030 are IMF projections and are marked as such on the charts. Design decisions are scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Ending the emissions window in 2017 and starting it in 2000 (Score: −1.5). The emissions data runs from 1995 to 2018, but emissions increased about 2.8% in 2018 (4.85 to 4.99 billion). Cutting that last year lets the line end at its lowest point. This gives the headline its largest drop: -17% vs -15% through 2018. Additionally, starting in 2000 also hdes that 2017 is only 6% below 1995, making the peak of 2005 read as a large change rather than just a small increase.
  • Truncated y-axes on both charts (Score: −1). The emissions chart starts at 4.4 billion tonnes and the coal chart at 0.4% of GDP. On zero-based axes, 2005-2017 emissions decline about 16% of the plot height, on ours, it fills about 60%. On the coal chart, between 2015 and 2022 there is a 35% decline in plot height. We kept the tick labels evenly spaced and clearly labeled, so a careful reader can recover the true scale. Line charts are typically allowed a non-zero baseline, which is what makes the choice feel normal. Zero-based versions made both declines look like gental slopes that do not support the titles.
  • The "6.9 billion tonnes avoided" calculated field (Score: −1). For every year after 2005 we calculated the gap between that year's emissions and the peak from 2005. Then we added up the gaps, and shaded the area green. The word "avoided" creates the assumption that otherwise, emissions would have stayed at an all-time high each year. We made sure to enforce that wording by leaving out the dip during 2009 (-7.7% in one year). It was driven, mostly, by the Great Recession rather than climate policy. We also considered creating a bar chart that showed the changes year-over-year, but decided on a line graph.
  • Production-based fossil-fuel CO₂, labeled precisely (Score: +1.5). We plotted "CO₂ embodied in production", which is the territorial measure that national inventories and climate agreements use, so the series itself was not cherry-picked. After feedback on our checkpoint, the title, subtitle, axis and source note all say "fossil-fuel CO₂" or "fuel combustion", because that is what the OECD series measured. This narrows down our claim to what the data can support. The measure is still incomplete as it leaves out the CO₂ released abroad to make goods Americans import, which is the gap the against chart exploits.
  • Selective annotation using the color green (Score: −0.5). We only labeled the 2005 peak and the 2017 endpoint. Additionally, we drew a dotted "2005 level" line so every later year reads as an improvement on the worst year. We deliberately left the 2009 dip (−7.7% in a single year) unlabeled since it was driven largely by the Great Recession rather than climate policy, and naming it would have handed readers a less flattering explanation for the decline. We used green since it is what most people think of when they think of environmental progress, and we added a subtitle stressing "every year since" to reinforce the idea that the problem is behind us.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Measuring the CO₂ embodied in imports, with exports as the baseline (Score: +1.5). When Americans buy things made in other countries, CO₂ is released over there to make them. Experts really do measure this, and official U.S. totals really do leave it out. We also showed exports so the comparison is fair: exports stay flat at about 600 million tonnes while imports grow, so the U.S. brings in much more of this CO₂ than it sends out. The note "None of this imported CO₂ is counted in official U.S. emissions totals" is true. It suggests the drop in the FOR chart comes from how CO₂ is counted, not from real progress.
  • Splicing two estimation methods into one series (Score: −2). The numbers for 1995–2018 come from one source (the OECD), and the numbers for 2019–2021 come from a different one (the IMF) that estimates CO₂ in a different way. Where the two meet, imports jump 66% in one year (1,362 → 2,260 million tonnes). That jump comes from switching methods, not from a real change. With OECD data alone, imports grow only about 1.4 times. Adding the IMF years makes it 2.2 times, which is where the title's "more than doubled" comes from. We list both sources but never say where one ends and the other begins, so most readers would never notice. This is the most misleading choice in our project.
  • Five-year bins with a two-year final bin (Score: −1). We averaged the years into groups of five. This is a normal way to smooth out ups and downs, like the trade drop in 2009. But it also hides the sudden jump in 2019, so it looks like a smooth step instead. The last bar only covers two years (2020–2021), both from the second source, yet it looks just like the five-year bars. We first made a line chart with one point per year, but the jump looked suspicious there, so we picked bars instead.
  • A 1995–99 reference line and a "×2.2" callout (Score:−0.5). The dotted line shows the level of the first bar, so every later bar looks like "more than before." The "×2.2" label compares the smallest bar to the biggest one. This skips over the 10% drop between 2005–09 and 2010–14, which would have hurt the "keeps going up" story. The bars do start at zero, so their heights are honest. The slant comes from which comparison we chose to point out.
  • Red versus gray, and the word "hidden" (Score: −0.5). Imports are bright red and exports are plain gray, so your eye goes straight to the growing red bars and sees them as a warning. Calling the CO₂ "hidden" makes it sound like someone is keeping it secret, but the data is public. It is just counted in a different way. After feedback on our checkpoint, the title, subtitle, and axis now clearly say "fossil-fuel CO₂," so the slant comes from word choice and color, not from confusion about what is being measured.

Final Reflection

The simplest part of our project was finding two stories that contradict one another. Both charts came from the same IMF/OECD table, and each number on the charts is accurate. After 2005, emissions produced in the United States did truly fall and the CO₂ associated with U.S. imports is large and continuing to grow. We discovered that persuasion was largely between which choices we made, such as which columns get plotted, what times (years) to include, what the baseline should be, and what the overall comparison should be. The difficult part was really trying to figure out how we should be deceptive while still keeping it subtle and not easily identifiable. We used the feedback we got from the checkpoint to hide the deceptive techniques better. Our drafts displayed IMF projections for 2023-2030 in a sense where it seemed like it were measured data, and the graders identified it right away. We were also told that our original proposition stated "CO₂ emissions" while the data only covered fossil-fuel combustion, so we had to narrow that down. Overall, we learned that deceptions that are visible on the chart get noticed while the deceptions that are left out mostly do not.

The most surprising thing for us was how much our truthful decisions helped our deceptive ones. Doing things correctly, like labelling tick marks correctly, starting the bar chart at zero, and using precise labels all helped establish our credibility. This credibility is what let us get away with our truncated time window as well as our spliced series. Some of our misleading choices also felt natural, like when we chose to call the shaded area "avoided emissions", or when we called imported carbon "hidden", it made it seem like normal headlines until we had to defend our claims. Since the charts come to opposing conclusions without using falsified data, we learned that the ethics of a visualization lie between the chart and the claim it supports, and not only the quantitative data.

We define ethical analysis and visualization as design where the conclusion would still hold up even after the reader learns about every choice behind it. This gives us a way to separate acceptable persuasion from misleading decisions. Choices that only focus on something true (color, annotations, titles, etc.) are acceptable because disclosing them does not change the data's conclusion. Misleading data is defined by choices that change what the data says. To be specific, cutting the time window to hide a reversal or splicing different methods into one series, or even showing forecasts as if they were measured data. For example, if adding one honest sentence to any of our captions would contradict the argument, then that choice is deceptive. However, the line is not always clear. A truncated axis on a line chart is usually reasonable (and common), but on our FOR chart, it had the same effect as our exaggerated one. That is why we believe intent and disclosure matter just as much as any rule on color or axes.
