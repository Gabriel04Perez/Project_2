Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Gabriel Perez, Gian Robles, Vedika Goyal, Mark Bishoff — gip005@ucsd.edu, gcrobles@ucsd.edu, vngoyal@ucsd.edu, mbishoff@ucsd.edu

Proposition: U.S. CO₂ emissions from burning fossil fuels are no longer a growing climate problem.

Dataset: IMF Climate Change Indicators, United States. Each visualization stacks two charts built from two of its indicators. The top chart uses CO₂ Emissions embodied in Domestic Final Demand, Production, and Trade, which comes from the OECD Trade in Embodied CO₂ (TeCO₂) database and counts CO₂ from fuel combustion only. The bottom chart uses Fossil Fuel Subsidies (IMF, 2023 update), whose implicit subsidies include the unpriced climate cost of each fuel's CO₂; its values for 2023–2030 are IMF projections and are marked as such on the charts. Design decisions are scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Ending the emissions window in 2017 and starting it in 2000 (Score: −1.5). The emissions data runs from 1995 to 2018, but emissions rose 2.8% in 2018 (4.85 → 4.99 billion tonnes). Stopping one year early lets the line end at its lowest point and gives the headline its largest possible drop: −17%, versus −15% through 2018. Starting in 2000 also hides that 2017 is only 6% below 1995, so the 2005 peak reads as a high plateau rather than a short bump. We tried the full 1995–2018 range, but it ended on an uptick and invited a "we're just back to 1990s levels" reading. Few readers know where the data actually stops, which makes this cut hard to catch and is why we score it as deceptive.
  • Truncated y-axes on both charts (Score: −1). The emissions chart starts at 4.4 billion tonnes and the coal chart at 0.4% of GDP. On zero-based axes, the 2005–2017 emissions decline would fill about 16% of the plot height and the 2015–2022 coal decline about 31%; here they fill about 60% and 53%, so moderate declines read as steep falls. We kept the tick labels evenly spaced and clearly labeled, so a careful reader can recover the true scale. Line charts are conventionally allowed a non-zero baseline, which is exactly what makes the choice feel normal. Zero-based versions made both declines look like gentle slopes that undercut the titles.
  • The "6.9 billion tonnes avoided" calculated field (Score: −1). For every year after 2005 we computed the gap between that year's emissions and the 2005 peak, summed the gaps, and shaded the area green, a conventional color for environmental progress. The arithmetic is correct, but the word "avoided" quietly assumes that emissions would otherwise have stayed at their all-time high every year, and it implies deliberate action. We reinforced that reading by leaving the 2009 dip (−7.7% in a single year) unlabeled: it was driven largely by the Great Recession rather than climate policy, and naming it would have handed readers a less flattering explanation. We considered a bar chart of year-over-year changes, but a single big cumulative number was more memorable.
  • Pairing emissions with coal subsidies, measured as a share of GDP (Score: −1.5). Stacking a cost chart under the emissions chart makes two different measures read as corroborating evidence, and the shared headline treats both as one "carbon problem". The link is real, since the IMF's implicit subsidies include the climate cost of coal's CO₂ (and the subtitle says so), but the chart cannot separate that cost from air pollution and the other components. The panel also shows coal alone: natural gas, which largely replaced coal, rose from 0.37% to 0.47% of GDP over 2015–2022 and is left out. Measuring as a share of GDP flatters the trend too: in constant 2021 dollars, coal's decline through 2022 is 26%, not 35%, because the economy grew. We tried a coal-plus-gas panel, but it barely moved (−4% in dollars), which would have weakened the argument.
  • Naming the measures precisely and marking the projections (Score: +1). The emissions chart plots "CO₂ embodied in production", the territorial measure that national inventories use, so it is not cherry-picked, just incomplete: it leaves out the CO₂ released abroad to make goods Americans import, which the AGAINST visualization exploits. After checkpoint feedback, every title, axis and note names its quantity as fossil-fuel or fuel-combustion CO₂, narrowing our claim to what the data supports. In the coal chart, the IMF projections for 2023–2030 are shaded, labeled, drawn dashed and end in a hollow marker, and the actual change (−35% by 2022) gets its own label; the overall headline's coal claim rests on actual years only. We score this +1 rather than +2 because the coal chart's own title, "on track to be cut nearly in half by 2030", still leans on the forecast.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Measuring the CO₂ embodied in imports, with exports as the baseline (Score: +1.5). Counting the CO₂ emitted abroad to make what Americans buy is an established method (the OECD TeCO₂ database exists to measure it), and it directly answers the FOR emissions chart: territorial inventories really do leave this CO₂ out. Showing exports next to imports keeps the comparison fair, since it shows that the U.S. is a large net importer of embodied carbon (exports stay flat near 600 million tonnes), not just that trade grew. The annotation "None of this imported CO₂ is counted in official U.S. emissions totals" is true, and it reframes the FOR emissions chart's decline as an accounting artifact rather than progress.
  • Splicing two estimation methods into one series (Score: −2). Values for 1995–2018 come from the OECD TeCO₂ database, while 2019–2021 come from a different IMF extension built on trade statistics. At the seam, imports jump 66% in a single year (1,362 → 2,260 million tonnes), which reflects a change in method rather than a real surge. Using OECD years alone, the period average rises only about 1.4×; including the spliced years pushes the last bar to 2.2×, which is where the title's "more than doubled" comes from. The source note cites both datasets but never says where one stops, so only a reader who opens the raw data would notice. This is the most deceptive choice in our project.
  • Five-year bins with a two-year final bin (Score: −1). Averaging years into periods is a calculated grouping that legitimately damps year-to-year noise, such as the 2009 trade collapse. Here it also hides the 2019 break: the 66% one-year jump becomes a smooth step between bars. The last bin averages only 2020–2021, both from the spliced source, yet it is drawn exactly like the five-year bins. Our checkpoint also had a yearly line version of this chart, but the one-year spike looked suspicious there, so we chose the binned bars.
  • Natural gas alone, indexed against GDP (Score: −1). The bottom chart shows natural gas by itself. Gas grew mostly by replacing coal: the two together cost 4% less in constant dollars in 2022 than in 2015, which the chart never shows. Indexing to 2015 = 100 makes gas's rise look dramatic while hiding its size, about $111 billion of a $757 billion total in 2022 (15%). The GDP line is a calculated field: we backed GDP out of the data as total subsidies divided by their share of GDP, which gives a fair yardstick for "faster than the economy". Following checkpoint feedback, the 2023–2030 projections are shaded, labeled and dashed, and the "Up 44% by 2022, 3× faster" callout uses actual years only; that disclosure keeps this at −1, though the chart's title still relies on the 2030 forecast for "double".
  • Emphasis and wording: a reference line, the "×2.2" callout, red, and "hidden" (Score: −0.5). The dotted 1995–99 line makes every later bar read as "more than then", and the ×2.2 callout compares the lowest period with the highest, skipping the 10% decline from 2005–09 to 2010–14. Imports and gas costs are drawn in alarm red against neutral gray, so the eye lands on whatever is growing. "Hidden", used in both chart titles, implies concealment, when the data is published and simply counted under a different convention. The bars start at zero and every label names its quantity as fossil-fuel CO₂ or its cost, so the slant lives in emphasis and word choice rather than in the numbers themselves.

Final Reflection

The most straightforward part of the project was finding two opposing stories. Both of our visualizations draw on the same IMF dataset, and every number on them is accurate: emissions produced inside the U.S. really did fall after 2005, and coal's subsidies really are shrinking, while the CO₂ embodied in U.S. imports and the cost of natural gas really are growing. Persuasion turned out to be mostly a matter of selection: which column to plot, which years to include, where to put the baseline, and which comparison to spell out in large type. The hard part was keeping the deceptive choices subtle. Our checkpoint feedback showed this clearly. Two of our draft charts drew IMF projections for 2023–2030 as if they were measured data, and reviewers caught it at once; in the final versions, which now form the bottom half of each visualization, those years are shaded, labeled and dashed, and our headline claims rest on actual years. They also flagged that our original proposition said "CO₂ emissions" when the data only covers fossil-fuel combustion, so we narrowed it. Meanwhile, the feedback never mentioned the FOR emissions chart's missing 2018 or the method seam inside the AGAINST imports chart's last bar. Deceptions that are drawn on the chart get noticed; deceptions in what is left out mostly do not.

What surprised us most was how much our earnest choices helped our deceptive ones. Cited sources, honest tick labels, a zero-based bar chart, clearly marked projections and a precise "fossil-fuel CO₂" label all signal trustworthiness, and that trust is what lets a truncated window or a spliced series slip past. We were also surprised by how natural some slanted choices felt while we made them. Calling the shaded area "avoided" emissions, or calling imported carbon "hidden", seemed like ordinary headline writing until we tried to defend each word against the data. The two charts reach opposite conclusions without a single false number, which convinced us that the ethics of a visualization lie in the relationship between the chart and the claim it is used to support, not in the numbers alone.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization as design whose conclusion would survive the reader learning about every choice behind it. That gives us a working boundary. Choices that only emphasize something true, like color, annotation, a framing title or picking one legitimate measure over another, are acceptable persuasion, because disclosing them does not change what the data says. Choices that change what the data appears to say are misleading: cutting the time window to hide a reversal, splicing incompatible methods into one series, drawing forecasts as observations, or stating a counterfactual as fact. A simple test is whether adding one honest sentence to the caption ("data continues to 2018, when emissions rose"; "2019–2021 use a different method") would collapse the argument. If it would, the choice is deceptive. The boundary is still blurry: a truncated axis on a line chart is conventional and often reasonable, yet its effect on our FOR emissions chart was the same as a deliberately exaggerated one. For that reason we think intent and disclosure matter as much as any rule about axes or colors.
