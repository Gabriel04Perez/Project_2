Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Gabriel Perez, Gian Robles, Vedika Goyal, Mark Bishoff — gip005@ucsd.edu, gcrobles@ucsd.edu, vngoyal@ucsd.edu, mbishoff@ucsd.edu

Proposition: U.S. CO₂ emissions from burning fossil fuels are no longer a growing climate problem.

Dataset: IMF Climate Change Indicators, United States. Each visualization stacks two charts built from two of its indicators. The top chart uses CO₂ Emissions embodied in Domestic Final Demand, Production, and Trade, which comes from the OECD Trade in Embodied CO₂ (TeCO₂) database and counts CO₂ from fuel combustion only. The bottom chart uses Fossil Fuel Subsidies (IMF, 2023 update), whose implicit subsidies include the unpriced climate cost of each fuel's CO₂; its values for 2023–2030 are IMF projections and are marked as such on the charts. Design decisions are scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Ending the emissions window in 2017 and starting it in 2000 (Score: −1.5). The emissions data runs from 1995 to 2018, but emissions increased about 2.8% in 2018 (4.85 to 4.99 billion). Cutting that last year, lets the line end at its lowest point. Giving the headline its largest drop: -17%, vs -15% through 2018. Starting in 2000 also hides that 2017 is ONLY 6% below 1995, the peak of 2005 reads high plateau rather than a small increase.
  • Truncated y-axes on both charts (Score: −1). The emissions chart starts at 4.4 billion tonnes and the coal chart at 0.4% of GDP. On zero-based axes. 2005-2017 emission decline about 16 % of the plot height. Then 2015–2022 coal decline about 35% of the plot height. We kept the tick labels evenly spaced and clearly labeled, so a careful reader can recover the true scale. Line charts are conventionally allowed a non-zero baseline, which is exactly what makes the choice feel normal. Zero-based versions made both declines look like gentle slopes that undercut the titles.
  • The "6.9 billion tonnes avoided" calculated field (Score: −1). For every year after 2005 we computed the gap between that year's emissions and the 2005 peak, summed the gaps, and shaded the area green, a conventional color for environmental progress. During the math, the word "avoided" assumes that emissions would otherwise have stayed at their all-time high every year. We ensure to enforce that wording by leaving the 2009 dip. Which was -7.7% in a single year, unlabeled. It was mostly driven by the Great Recession rather than climate policy. We considered a bar chart of year-over-year changes, but a single big cumulative number was more memorable.
  • Pairing emissions with coal subsidies, measured as a share of GDP (Score: −1.5). Pairing a cost chart with an emission chart makes two different measures read as evidence, and the headlines shared as one "carbon problem". The connection is real, since the IMF's implicit subsidies include the climate cost of coal's CO₂, but the chart cannot separate that cost from air pollution. The panel also shows coal alone: natural gas, which largely replaced coal, rose from 0.37% to 0.47% of GDP over 2015–2022 and is left out. Measuring as a share of GDP flatters the trend too: in constant 2021 dollars, coal's decline through 2022 is 26%, not 35%, because the economy grew.
  • Naming the measures precisely and marking the projections (Score: +1). The emissions chart plots "CO₂ embodied in production", the territorial measure that national inventories use. It leaves out the CO₂ released abroad to make goods Americans import, which the AGAINST visualization exploits. In the coal chart, the IMF projections for 2023–2030 are shaded, labeled, drawn dashed and end in a hollow marker, and the actual change (−35% by 2022) gets its own label; the overall headline's coal claim rests on actual years only. We score this +1 rather than +2 because the coal chart's own title, "on track to be cut nearly in half by 2030", still leans on the forecast.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Measuring the CO₂ embodied in imports, with exports as the baseline (Score: +1.5). When Americans buy things made in other countries, CO₂ is released over there to make them. Experts really do measure this, and official U.S. totals really do leave it out. We also showed exports so the comparison is fair: exports stay flat at about 600 million tonnes while imports grow, so the U.S. brings in much more of this CO₂ than it sends out. The note "None of this imported CO₂ is counted in official U.S. emissions totals" is true. It suggests the drop in the FOR chart comes from how CO₂ is counted, not from real progress.
  • Splicing two estimation methods into one series (Score: −2). The numbers for 1995–2018 come from one source (the OECD), and the numbers for 2019–2021 come from a different one (the IMF) that estimates CO₂ in a different way. Where the two meet, imports jump 66% in one year (1,362 → 2,260 million tonnes). That jump comes from switching methods, not from a real change. With OECD data alone, imports grow only about 1.4 times. Adding the IMF years makes it 2.2 times, which is where the title's "more than doubled" comes from. We list both sources but never say where one ends and the other begins, so most readers would never notice. This is the most misleading choice in our project.
  • Five-year bins with a two-year final bin (Score: −1). We averaged the years into groups of five. This is a normal way to smooth out ups and downs, like the trade drop in 2009. But it also hides the sudden jump in 2019, so it looks like a smooth step instead. The last bar only covers two years (2020–2021), both from the second source, yet it looks just like the five-year bars. We first made a line chart with one point per year, but the jump looked suspicious there, so we picked bars instead.
  • A 1995–99 reference line and a "×2.2" callout (Score: −0.5). The dotted line shows the level of the first bar, so every later bar looks like "more than before." The "×2.2" label compares the smallest bar to the biggest one. This skips over the 10% drop between 2005–09 and 2010–14, which would have hurt the "keeps going up" story. The bars do start at zero, so their heights are honest. The slant comes from which comparison we chose to point out.
  • Red versus gray, and the word "hidden" (Score: −0.5). Imports are bright red and exports are plain gray, so your eye goes straight to the growing red bars and sees them as a warning. Calling the CO₂ "hidden" makes it sound like someone is keeping it secret, but the data is public. It is just counted in a different way. After feedback on our checkpoint, the title, subtitle, and axis now clearly say "fossil-fuel CO₂," so the slant comes from word choice and color, not from confusion about what is being measured.

Final Reflection

The most straightforward part of the project was finding two opposing stories. Both of our visualizations draw on the same IMF dataset, and every number on them is accurate: emissions produced inside the U.S. really did fall after 2005, and coal's subsidies really are shrinking, while the CO₂ embodied in U.S. imports and the cost of natural gas really are growing. Persuasion turned out to be mostly a matter of selection: which column to plot, which years to include, where to put the baseline, and which comparison to spell out in large type. The hard part was keeping the deceptive choices subtle. Our checkpoint feedback showed this clearly. Two of our draft charts drew IMF projections for 2023–2030 as if they were measured data, and reviewers caught it at once; in the final versions, which now form the bottom half of each visualization, those years are shaded, labeled and dashed, and our headline claims rest on actual years. They also flagged that our original proposition said "CO₂ emissions" when the data only covers fossil-fuel combustion, so we narrowed it. Meanwhile, the feedback never mentioned the FOR emissions chart's missing 2018 or the method seam inside the AGAINST imports chart's last bar. Deceptions that are drawn on the chart get noticed; deceptions in what is left out mostly do not.

What surprised us most was how much our earnest choices helped our deceptive ones. Cited sources, honest tick labels, a zero-based bar chart, clearly marked projections and a precise "fossil-fuel CO₂" label all signal trustworthiness, and that trust is what lets a truncated window or a spliced series slip past. We were also surprised by how natural some slanted choices felt while we made them. Calling the shaded area "avoided" emissions, or calling imported carbon "hidden", seemed like ordinary headline writing until we tried to defend each word against the data. The two charts reach opposite conclusions without a single false number, which convinced us that the ethics of a visualization lie in the relationship between the chart and the claim it is used to support, not in the numbers alone.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization as design whose conclusion would survive the reader learning about every choice behind it. That gives us a working boundary. Choices that only emphasize something true, like color, annotation, a framing title or picking one legitimate measure over another, are acceptable persuasion, because disclosing them does not change what the data says. Choices that change what the data appears to say are misleading: cutting the time window to hide a reversal, splicing incompatible methods into one series, drawing forecasts as observations, or stating a counterfactual as fact. A simple test is whether adding one honest sentence to the caption ("data continues to 2018, when emissions rose"; "2019–2021 use a different method") would collapse the argument. If it would, the choice is deceptive. The boundary is still blurry: a truncated axis on a line chart is conventional and often reasonable, yet its effect on our FOR emissions chart was the same as a deliberately exaggerated one. For that reason we think intent and disclosure matter as much as any rule about axes or colors.
